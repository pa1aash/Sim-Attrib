\begin{abstract}
Attributing a simulator's misspecification to one of its components, rather than merely
detecting that the simulator is misspecified, presupposes that the available summaries can
tell the components apart -- a precondition that can fail quietly. We check it, using
established rank-and-condition-number diagnostics rather than proposing new ones, on a
canonical three-component compartmental epidemic simulator under a realistic adversarial
threat model. Under a scope assumption of at most one distortion parameter per component, one
natural summary set separates all three components across every one of eight ways two
declared distortion families can be assigned to them; the same measurement locates exactly
where this breaks once a component is allowed two distortion parameters at once, on a real,
interpretable confound between two mechanisms rather than an estimator or threshold artifact.
We then evaluate a natural selective-inference construction, composed entirely from
established exact-inference tools, for turning this diagnostic into a calibrated attribution
test: it is comfortably affordable at a known parameter and fails to terminate once the
nuisance parameters are only known within a realistic neighborhood. The boundary of that
failure is predicted, before it is measured, from the ratio of nuisance-induced shift to
observation noise, and the prediction holds -- giving a check transportable to other
simulators of this class. Every tool used is prior art; the contribution is the finding.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A simulator used for scientific inference is essentially always misspecified somewhere.
Existing tests for this in simulation-based inference ask whether it is misspecified at all;
a practitioner who gets a positive answer usually wants to know \emph{which part} of the
simulator to fix, not only that something needs fixing. Attributing misspecification to a
specific component of a multi-part simulator presupposes something the detection question
does not need: that the available summary statistics can actually tell the components apart.
That precondition can fail quietly, and a procedure that reports a confident attribution
without checking it first is reporting a number the data may not support.

This paper checks that precondition, using established tools rather than proposing new ones,
on a canonical case -- a three-component compartmental epidemic simulator, with transmission,
progression, and observation as the components -- and reports four findings, none a claim
about a new method (Section~\ref{sec:background}):
\begin{itemize}[topsep=1pt,itemsep=0pt,parsep=0pt,leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Where attribution is identifiable.} A rank-and-condition-number screen, under a
realistic adversarial threat model rather than only a favorable one, shows one summary set
separates all three components under every one of eight distortion assignments tried
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).
\item \textbf{Where it stops being identifiable.} The same measurement locates exactly where
this breaks, once a component carries two distortion parameters at once, on a specific,
interpretable confound rather than an estimator, threshold, or structural artifact
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).
\item \textbf{Where a natural selective-inference construction on this fails.} Composing
established exact-inference tools gives a procedure controlling the selective type-I error at
the nominal level, conservatively; we find it comfortably affordable at a known parameter but
unable to terminate once the nuisance parameters are only known within a realistic
neighborhood (Section~\ref{sec:negative-result}).
\item \textbf{Why, transportably.} The nuisance-to-noise ratio governing that failure is
predicted before it is measured, and the prediction holds (Section~\ref{sec:negative-result}).
\end{itemize}

This is a paper about where component-level misspecification attribution is, and is not,
identifiable under a realistic threat model, and why a natural construction for the
identifiable case has a boundary that can be predicted rather than only discovered.

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:background}

\begin{itemize}[topsep=1pt,itemsep=0pt,parsep=0pt,leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Detecting misspecification versus attributing it.} SBI has moved from asking
whether a simulator is misspecified to asking where.
\citet{montel2025testsmodelmisspecificationsimulationbased} give a family of tests, from local
distortions to global model checks, for detecting misspecification of an SBI posterior; their
global test corrects for multiplicity via a trials-corrected $p$-value on the \emph{minimum} of
per-summary statistics, controlling the global null that the simulator is correctly specified
\emph{somewhere} rather than the identity of the arg\,min, and their presentation is framed as
detection rather than component-level attribution. We take this as the baseline our diagnostic
sits downstream of: before any attribution procedure runs, it is worth asking whether the
summaries in hand can distinguish the components being attributed to.
\item \textbf{The identifiability precondition.} That question has an exact answer in a closely
related setting. \citet{Kahl_2019} show per-component input reconstruction in a dynamic system
is identifiable iff a structural sensitivity map has full column rank, resting on the
invertibility condition of \citet{Sain_1969}; \citet{CatchpoleMorgan_1997} give the general
parameter-redundancy framework this instantiates, and \citet{Brynjarsd_ttir_2014} frame the
broader identifiability problem for model discrepancy in computer-model calibration. We treat
all four as an established precondition, not a result of this paper: our diagnostic decides
whether the precondition holds for a given simulator and summary set, not whether it is right.
\item \textbf{Diagnostic tooling, applied rather than proposed.} Screening a sensitivity
matrix's rank and condition number to decide whether parameters are jointly recoverable is not
new; \citet{Cintr_n_Arias_2009} apply exactly this screen to a compartmental epidemic model,
the class we use in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}. Because our sensitivity matrix is
simulation-estimated rather than symbolic, we also need to know how far a finite-difference
estimate can be trusted under simulation noise; \citet{Mor__2011} and \citet{Mor__2012} give
the relevant noise-estimation and near-optimal-step-size results, of which our step-size sweep
(Section~\ref{sec:method}) is a coarser, pre-registered substitute. Whether the resulting
spectrum has a clean gap or decays smoothly across many decades -- where any rank tolerance is
partly a statement about where the analyst put it -- is the objection
\citet{Gutenkunst_2007} raise for sensitivity spectra in systems-biology models generally;
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} measures rather than assumes an answer.
\item \textbf{Exact inference under a composite, simulation-only null.}
Section~\ref{sec:negative-result} evaluates a natural construction for turning the diagnostic's
output into a calibrated test: exact finite-sample inference under a null depending on unknown
nuisance parameters, by maximizing a simulated $p$-value over the nuisance set, is
\citet{Dufour_2006}'s maximized Monte Carlo test; calibrating a selection event with no
analytic description, by rejection-sampling the simulator's own null until a replicate lands in
the observed cell, is available for arbitrary conditioning events following
\citet{freidling2026selectiverandomizationinferenceadaptive}. Composing the two lets a
simulator supply everything both need without further modelling assumptions; we cite both as
established and evaluate the composition, rather than propose either as new.
\end{itemize}

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

Figure~\ref{fig:method} (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}) summarizes the procedure below end to end.

\paragraph{Scope assumption, stated first.}
Throughout this paper we assume each simulator component is misspecified by at most one
mechanism at a time: every component carries a single one-parameter distortion family, never
two at once. This is load-bearing rather than a footnote (Section~\ref{sec:experiments} shows
what happens once it is dropped), so we state it before the diagnostic's machinery.

\paragraph{The diagnostic.}
Every ingredient below is prior art, applied rather than proposed (Section~\ref{sec:background}).
Declare the simulator as $K$ components, each carrying a one-parameter distortion family
$\delta_k(\cdot\,;\eta_k)$ bit-identical to the base simulator at $\eta_k = 0$. Estimate the
summary Jacobian $J = \partial s/\partial\eta$ at $\eta=0$ by central differences under common
random numbers, swept over six decades of step size $h$ (without common random numbers the
finite difference is dominated by noise of order $\sigma/h$ and no stable range exists,
confirmed by a negative control). Summaries are divided by their prior-predictive standard
deviation and each $\eta_k$ by a fixed relative perturbation scale -- both, and every tolerance
below, fixed before any singular value existed. Components are \emph{separable} if $J$ has full
column rank at tolerance $\tau$ and condition number $\kappa \le \kappa_{\max}$; we use
$\tau=10^{-2}$, $\kappa_{\max}=1/\tau=100$, from a compute budget -- resolving components to
$\kappa$ costs order $\kappa^2$ replicates, and $\kappa=100$ exhausts this study's budget. A
singular value varying by more than a factor of two across the plateau is \emph{unresolved} and
counted toward the rank in neither direction, a third outcome distinct from separable/not.

\paragraph{Where separability fails.}
When the rank condition fails, we report the \emph{equivalence class} named by each near-null
right singular vector $v$ ($\sigma \le \tau\sigma_1$) rather than a single culprit: component
$k$ joins if $|v_k|\ge 0.3$, comfortably below both the two-component ($\approx 0.707$) and
three-component ($\approx 0.577$) confound values. Coherence is reported for interpretation but
is not the decision rule -- the coherence consistent with $\kappa_{\max}=100$ is
$\mu\approx0.9998$, too close to one to estimate, so we use it only to point at which column
pair is responsible. No arrow leaves the verdict: the diagnostic decides whether attribution is
well posed, and nothing further.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\paragraph{Simulator and summary sets.}
We apply the diagnostic to a $K=3$ compartmental epidemic simulator (Figure~\ref{fig:simulator},
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}):
transmission, progression, and observation, each carrying one declared distortion family that
is the base simulator exactly at $\eta_k=0$, under two declared family sets (base and
adversarial, the latter designed so all three columns perturb the epidemic curve's growth
rate). We evaluate three summary sets: $S_A$, epidemic-curve features a domain modeller would
choose (peak height, peak time, final size, growth rate); $S_B$, roughly ten time-binned
incidence counts an SBI practitioner would choose; and $S_C$, an impoverished positive control
with two summaries, which \emph{must} fail by construction ($d=2<K=3$) and does -- rank 2,
$\kappa=\infty$ -- confirming genuine rank deficiency rather than a harness artifact. A
random-attributor floor of $1/K$ (analytic $0.3333$, measured $0.3299$ over $10{,}000$ draws)
is reported against every accuracy figure elsewhere in this line of work.

\paragraph{Where attribution is identifiable: $S_B$ under eight family assignments.}
Combining the two declared families component-wise gives eight three-column distortion models
(Figure~\ref{fig:assignments}), only two of which -- all-base and all-adversarial -- were
originally declared; the other six recombine the same columns at no additional simulation cost.
Under our scope assumption, $S_B$ separates all three components under \emph{all eight}:
condition number $6.628$ to $65.64$, every singular value resolved, no coherence pair flagged.
$S_A$ is a control, not a result: it splits exactly on the transmission family, failing at
$\kappa=100.9$ -- $0.9\%$ past the ceiling -- on the assignment that swaps in only the
adversarial transmission distortion. An instrument that always reported ``separable'' could
not produce that contrast. The margin is not uniform: $S_B$'s verdict survives $\tau$ moving by
$9.881\times$ under the base assignment but only $1.523\times$ under the tightest one
(Figure~\ref{fig:threshold}, Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}), so doubling $\tau$ -- this project's
own coarse robustness check -- flips it.

\paragraph{Where it is not: two distortion parameters per component.}
Placing all six declared distortion directions side by side -- two per component -- gives a
single six-column object. For $S_B$ it is \textbf{inseparable} at $\kappa=628.9$, rank $4$ of
$6$ (Figure~\ref{fig:spectrum}): not an unresolved estimator (worst singular-value variation
across the plateau, $1.023$ against an admissible $2$); not a threshold artifact (inseparable
across $\tau$ from $0.005$ to $1.0$, with no tolerance at which the object is both separable
and affordable); and not structural ($d=10\ge6$). Both near-null directions are
cross-mechanism, naming progression together with observation, with transmission carrying
under $5\%$ of the energy in either (Figure~\ref{fig:confound}, Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}):
a drifting removal hazard is nearly indistinguishable from a constant hazard change combined
with a drifting reporting rate. The confound needs the progression component to carry two
distortion parameters at once, exactly why it cannot arise under our one-parameter-per-component
scope, and exactly why that restriction -- not a bound on $\kappa$ -- is what the assumption
has to be. At $K=6$ the spectrum matches the gapless, no-clean-break picture the sloppy-models
literature describes \citep{Gutenkunst_2007} (spread $2.80$ decades, gap prominence $1.40$,
$\tau\sigma_1$ falling inside the spectrum); at $K=3$ it does not. The reassurance a $K=3$
threshold offers is a fact about $K=3$, not about the simulator.

\section{The MMC negative result}
\label{sec:negative-result}

Composing Dufour's maximized Monte Carlo with rejection-sampling calibration
(Section~\ref{sec:background}) gives, on paper, conditional inference on which component is
attributed that controls the selective type-I error at the nominal level -- conservatively
rather than exactly, since the composition inherits both the supremum-over-nuisance-space and
the discrete-Monte-Carlo sources of conservativeness -- without ever characterizing the
selection event analytically. The composition's only theorem is a short exchangeability
argument requiring the selection rule to be a fixed, deterministic function of the data, and
it holds only if every admissible nuisance value keeps the observed selection event reachable
at all. We evaluate this construction on our own simulator rather than propose it.

\paragraph{Affordable at a known parameter, unbounded over the nuisance set.}
At the true parameter the composition is comfortably affordable: the worst selection cell
holds $23\%$ of null draws, and one test costs $4.2\times10^5$ to $4.3\times10^7$ simulator
draws against a pre-registered gate of $10^8$ -- a clear pass. Once the nuisance parameters
(transmission and recovery rates, reporting fraction, initial infections, observation noise
scale) are only known to lie in a relative box around that point, the same gate fails at every
declared design corner: at a relative half-width of $5\%$, zero acceptances occur in
$100{,}000$ null draws, a $95\%$ upper bound of $3.84\times10^{-5}$ on the acceptance
probability, pricing one test at no fewer than $2.6\times10^9$ draws. The acceptance
probability is not merely small, it is statistically indistinguishable from zero, and the
obstruction does not move with more compute.

\paragraph{The mechanism, and the boundary where it takes over.}
The selection rule's fixed-function requirement means it cannot track $\theta$; the nuisance
parameters shift the normalized summary vector by an amount growing with how far $\theta$ is
allowed to range, while a single draw's noise magnitude stays fixed. Once that shift exceeds
the noise, a $\theta$-free rule selects one component deterministically and the cell the data
actually selected becomes unreachable. A sweep over ten relative half-widths from $0.1\%$ to
$5\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:nontermination}) locates where this happens: the collapse is gradual
rather than abrupt (about one decade of acceptance probability lost per $0.7\%$ of relative
nuisance error, log-linear at $R^2=0.94$), and the pre-registered cost gate passes at every
declared corner only inside a $\pm0.5\%$ box on all five nuisance coordinates simultaneously.
The sweep's own script recorded, before it produced a number, a prediction of where the
nuisance shift would overtake the per-draw noise; the crossing falls between a relative
half-width of $0.5\%$ and $0.75\%$, exactly where the gate stops passing. The ratio of
nuisance shift to observation noise is a one-line check a reader with a different simulator
can run before attempting this composition, and it is the most transportable result here.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}[topsep=1pt,itemsep=0pt,parsep=0pt,leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{The scope assumption is not a formality.} Every positive result here holds under
the assumption, stated in Section~\ref{sec:method}, that each component carries at most one
distortion parameter -- load-bearing rather than conservative (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}):
the moment one component carries two, the same summary set that separated all three under
every one-parameter assignment we tried becomes inseparable on a real confound. We have not
tested compound misspecification within a single mechanism -- two distortions of the
\emph{same} component rather than of two different ones.
\item \textbf{The diagnostic's own status.} The screen we apply (Section~\ref{sec:method}) is
prior art, and the one seam it leaves unoccupied -- carrying an estimated simulation noise
level forward into the rank tolerance -- is disclosed rather than filled; we derive our
tolerance from a compute budget instead. Nothing here depends on occupying it, since the
tolerance is fixed before any singular value in this paper existed, but a reader adapting the
diagnostic where the noise level binds should not assume this paper solved that seam.
\item \textbf{One simulator, one scope of family sets, two hand-placed annotations.} Every
result here is measured on a single simulator under two distortion family sets chosen by us --
one deliberately adversarial; the eight assignments (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) are every
combination those sets permit, not a sample of families in general, and transport to other
simulator classes is untested. Two figures also carry hand-placed annotations outside the
automated check -- the rank on Figure~\ref{fig:spectrum}, the separability labels on
Figure~\ref{fig:assignments} -- both verified restatements, not independent claims.
\end{itemize}
