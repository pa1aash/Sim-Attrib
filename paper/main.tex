\begin{abstract}
Attributing a simulator's misspecification to one component presupposes that the available
summaries can tell the components apart -- a precondition that can fail quietly. We check it,
on a three-component epidemic simulator under an adversarial threat model, using
rank-and-condition-number diagnostics. One summary set separates all three components under
every one of eight distortion assignments tried, given at most one distortion parameter per
component; the same measurement locates exactly where this breaks once a component carries two
parameters at once ($\kappa=628.9$, rank 4 of 6), on a real, interpretable confound. A
selective-inference construction for turning this diagnostic into a calibrated attribution test
is affordable at a known parameter but fails to terminate once the nuisance parameters are only
known to the precision a maximum-likelihood fit to the data gives them -- a box wider, on every
coordinate, than one already shown to break it. The boundary is predicted before it is
measured, from the ratio of nuisance shift to observation noise, and the prediction holds.
Every tool used is prior art; the contribution is the finding.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A simulator used for scientific inference is essentially always misspecified somewhere.
Existing tests for this in simulation-based inference ask whether it is misspecified at all;
a practitioner who gets a positive answer usually wants to know \emph{which part} of the
simulator to fix, not only that something needs fixing. Attributing misspecification to a
specific component of a multi-part simulator presupposes something the detection question
does not need: that the available summary statistics can actually tell the components apart.
That precondition can fail quietly, and a procedure that reports a confident attribution
without checking it first is reporting a number the data may not support.

This paper checks that precondition, using established tools rather than proposing new ones,
on a canonical case -- a three-component compartmental epidemic simulator, with transmission,
progression, and observation as the components -- and reports four findings, none a claim
about a new method (Section~\ref{sec:background}):
\begin{itemize}[topsep=1pt,itemsep=0pt,parsep=0pt,leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Where attribution is identifiable.} Under a scope assumption of at most one
distortion parameter per component, a rank-and-condition-number screen shows one summary set
separates all three components under every one of eight distortion assignments tried, against a
realistic adversarial threat model rather than only a favorable one; a second, coarser summary
set fails on exactly the assignment the adversarial family targets, ruling out an instrument
that always reports separable (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).
\item \textbf{Where it stops being identifiable.} The same measurement locates exactly where
this breaks, once a component carries two distortion parameters at once, on a specific,
interpretable confound rather than an estimator, threshold, or structural artifact
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).
\item \textbf{Where a natural selective-inference construction on this fails.} Composing
established exact-inference tools gives a procedure controlling the selective type-I error at
the nominal level, conservatively; we find it comfortably affordable at a known parameter but
unable to terminate once the nuisance parameters are only known within a realistic
neighborhood (Section~\ref{sec:negative-result}).
\item \textbf{Why, transportably.} The nuisance-to-noise ratio governing that failure is
predicted before it is measured, and the prediction holds (Section~\ref{sec:negative-result}).
\end{itemize}

This is a paper about where component-level misspecification attribution is, and is not,
identifiable under a realistic threat model, and why a natural construction for the
identifiable case has a boundary that can be predicted rather than only discovered.

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:background}

\begin{itemize}[topsep=1pt,itemsep=0pt,parsep=0pt,leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Detecting misspecification versus attributing it.} SBI has moved from asking
whether a simulator is misspecified to asking where -- \citet{Cranmer_2020} name summary
informativeness and misspecification diagnosis among the field's open problems, and several
routes now answer the detection half. \citet{schmitt2022detectingmodelmisspecificationamortized}
flag out-of-distribution embeddings with a single discrepancy score; \citet{ward2022robustneuralposteriorestimation}
model the summary-level error explicitly and use it to make posterior estimation robust to it;
\citet{Frazier_2020} show ABC posteriors under misspecification concentrate on a pseudo-true
parameter and give a shrinking-tolerance diagnostic for detecting the concentration failing.
None resolves component identity: each is a global or per-summary signal that something is
wrong, not a claim about which of several components caused it -- the question this paper asks
instead. \citet{montel2025testsmodelmisspecificationsimulationbased} give a family of tests,
from local distortions to global model checks; their global test corrects for multiplicity via
a trials-corrected $p$-value on the \emph{minimum} of per-summary statistics, controlling the
global null that the simulator is correctly specified \emph{somewhere} rather than the identity
of the arg\,min, and their local tests run one distortion direction at a time without
cross-distortion disambiguation. We take this cluster as the baseline our diagnostic sits
downstream of: before any attribution procedure runs, it is worth asking whether the summaries
in hand can distinguish the components being attributed to. Run on our six-column confound
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}), Montel et al.'s global test would correctly reject the global
null -- the distortion is detectable -- but its arg\,min is exactly the ambiguity our diagnostic
reports as an equivalence class rather than a component; their local tests would independently
flag both confounded distortions as significant, since each is tested against the base
simulator without reference to the other. Neither resolves which of progression or observation
to name, because resolving it is not the question either construction asks.
\item \textbf{The identifiability precondition.} That question has an exact answer in a closely
related setting. \citet{Kahl_2019} show per-component input reconstruction in a dynamic system
is identifiable iff a structural sensitivity map has full column rank, resting on the
invertibility condition of \citet{Sain_1969}; \citet{CatchpoleMorgan_1997} give the general
parameter-redundancy framework this instantiates, and \citet{Brynjarsd_ttir_2014} frame the
broader identifiability problem for model discrepancy in computer-model calibration.
\citet{Miao_2011} give the differential-algebra route to the same question directly on
epidemic-ODE models: two parameters entering only through a fixed combination are structurally
non-identifiable regardless of design, exactly the failure mode our six-column confound
exhibits empirically; \citet{Raue_2009}'s profile likelihood is the numerical diagnostic for
the same failure under noisy, partially observed data -- a flat profile along the confounded
direction, where our near-null singular vector plays the equivalent role for a
simulation-estimated sensitivity map rather than a symbolic one. We treat all six as an
established precondition, not a result of this paper: our diagnostic decides whether the
precondition holds for a given simulator and summary set, not whether it is right.
\item \textbf{Diagnostic tooling, applied rather than proposed.} Screening a sensitivity
matrix's rank and condition number to decide whether parameters are jointly recoverable is not
new; \citet{Cintr_n_Arias_2009} apply exactly this screen to a compartmental epidemic model,
the class we use in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}. Because our sensitivity matrix is
simulation-estimated rather than symbolic, we also need to know how far a finite-difference
estimate can be trusted under simulation noise; \citet{Mor__2011} and \citet{Mor__2012} give
the relevant noise-estimation and near-optimal-step-size results, of which our step-size sweep
(Section~\ref{sec:method}) is a coarser, pre-registered substitute. Whether the resulting
spectrum has a clean gap or decays smoothly across many decades -- where any rank tolerance is
partly a statement about where the analyst put it -- is the objection
\citet{Gutenkunst_2007} raise for sensitivity spectra in systems-biology models generally;
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} measures rather than assumes an answer.
\item \textbf{Exact inference under a composite, simulation-only null.}
Section~\ref{sec:negative-result} evaluates a natural construction for turning the diagnostic's
output into a calibrated test: exact finite-sample inference under a null depending on unknown
nuisance parameters, by maximizing a simulated $p$-value over the nuisance set, is
\citet{Dufour_2006}'s maximized Monte Carlo test; calibrating a selection event with no
analytic description, by rejection-sampling the simulator's own null until a replicate lands in
the observed cell, is available for arbitrary conditioning events following
\citet{freidling2026selectiverandomizationinferenceadaptive}. Composing the two lets a
simulator supply everything both need without further modelling assumptions; we cite both as
established and evaluate the composition, rather than propose either as new. Dufour's is not
the only route through a composite null: \citet{xie2022reprosamplesmethodfinite}'s repro
samples profile a nuisance parameter through a data-driven candidate set, and
\citet{Barber_2022}'s approximate co-sufficient sampling conditions on a perturbed maximum
likelihood estimate to test a composite null without an exact sufficient statistic. Both
confirm the same structural point Dufour's construction rests on -- a composite null can be
discharged by conditioning out or maximizing over the nuisance direction rather than estimating
it away -- and both would, like Dufour's, absorb our two confounded distortion parameters
together when conditioning on the confounded direction, giving a valid test of whether the
six-column model fits without attributing a rejection to either parameter individually.
\end{itemize}

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Scope assumption, stated first.}
Throughout this paper we assume each simulator component is misspecified by at most one
mechanism at a time: every component carries a single one-parameter distortion family, never
two at once. This is load-bearing rather than a footnote (Section~\ref{sec:experiments} shows
what happens once it is dropped), so we state it before the diagnostic's machinery.

\paragraph{The diagnostic.}
Every ingredient below is prior art, applied rather than proposed (Section~\ref{sec:background}),
in four steps:
\begin{enumerate}[topsep=1pt,itemsep=1pt,parsep=0pt,leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Declare.} The simulator as $K$ components, each carrying a one-parameter distortion
family $\delta_k(\cdot\,;\eta_k)$ bit-identical to the base simulator at $\eta_k=0$, asserted
bit-for-bit before anything else runs.
\item \textbf{Simulate.} Estimate the summary Jacobian $J=\partial s/\partial\eta$ at $\eta=0$ by
central differences under common random numbers, swept over six decades of step size $h$;
without common random numbers the finite difference is dominated by noise of order $\sigma/h$
and no stable range exists, confirmed by a negative control.
\item \textbf{Normalise and take the SVD.} Summaries are divided by their prior-predictive
standard deviation and each $\eta_k$ by a fixed relative perturbation scale -- both, and every
tolerance below, fixed before any singular value existed. The diagnostic's only input is the
summary vector; it never receives a component identity or a ground-truth label, so there is no
hidden truth available to leak into its answer.
\item \textbf{Decide.} Components are \emph{separable} if $J$'s condition number satisfies
$\kappa \le \kappa_{\max}=1/\tau$ -- equivalently, full column rank at tolerance $\tau$, since
$\kappa \le 1/\tau$ restates rank deficiency at $\tau$ rather than adding a second, independent
requirement; we report both quantities because each is the more natural unit in different
places below. We use $\tau=10^{-2}$, $\kappa_{\max}=100$, from a compute budget: resolving
components to precision $\kappa$ in a Monte Carlo-estimated Jacobian costs order $\kappa^2$
replicates, since a singular value as small as $\sigma_1/\kappa$ is distinguishable from
sampling noise only once the per-entry standard error falls below it, and a
difference-quotient estimate's standard error falls as $(\text{replicates})^{-1/2}$; at
$\kappa=100$ this exhausts this study's budget. A singular value
varying by more than a factor of two across the plateau is \emph{unresolved} and counted toward
the rank in neither direction, a third outcome distinct from separable/not, so the reported rank
can be an interval rather than a single number. Resolution is a property of the
\emph{estimator}; whether the underlying spectrum has a clean gap or decays smoothly across many
decades is a property of the \emph{matrix}, and passing the resolution test answers the first
question, not the second (Section~\ref{sec:background}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Where separability fails.}
When the rank condition fails, we report the \emph{equivalence class} named by each near-null
right singular vector $v$ ($\sigma \le \tau\sigma_1$) rather than a single culprit: component
$k$ joins if $|v_k|\ge 0.3$, comfortably below both the two-component ($\approx 0.707$) and
three-component ($\approx 0.577$) confound values. Coherence is reported for interpretation but
is not the decision rule -- the coherence consistent with $\kappa_{\max}=100$ is
$\mu\approx0.9998$, too close to one to estimate, so we use it only to point at which column
pair is responsible. No arrow leaves the verdict: the diagnostic decides whether attribution is
well posed, and nothing further.

\paragraph{Simulator and summary sets.}
We apply the diagnostic to the $K=3$ compartmental epidemic simulator of Figure~\ref{fig:simulator}:
transmission, progression, and observation, each carrying one declared distortion family that
is the base simulator exactly at $\eta_k=0$, under two declared family sets (base and
adversarial, the latter designed so all three columns perturb the epidemic curve's growth
rate). We evaluate three summary sets: $S_A$, epidemic-curve features a domain modeller would
choose (peak height, peak time, final size, growth rate); $S_B$, roughly ten time-binned
incidence counts an SBI practitioner would choose; and $S_C$, an impoverished positive control
with two summaries, which \emph{must} fail by construction ($d=2<K=3$) and does -- rank 2,
$\kappa=\infty$ -- confirming genuine rank deficiency rather than a harness artifact. A
random-attributor floor of $1/K$ (analytic $0.3333$, measured $0.3299$ over $10{,}000$ draws)
is the baseline any component-attribution accuracy should be read against.

\paragraph{Where attribution is identifiable: $S_B$ under eight family assignments.}
Combining the two declared families component-wise gives eight three-column distortion models
(Figure~\ref{fig:assignments}), only two of which -- all-base and all-adversarial -- were
originally declared; the other six recombine the same columns at no additional simulation cost.
Under our scope assumption, $S_B$ separates all three components under \emph{all eight}:
condition number $6.628$ to $65.64$, every singular value resolved, no coherence pair flagged.
$S_A$ is a control, not a result: it splits exactly on the transmission family, failing at
$\kappa=100.9$ -- $0.9\%$ past the ceiling -- on the assignment that swaps in only the
adversarial transmission distortion. An instrument that always reported ``separable'' could
not produce that contrast. The margin is not uniform: $S_B$'s verdict survives $\tau$ moving by
$9.881\times$ under the base assignment but only $1.523\times$ under the tightest one
(Figure~\ref{fig:threshold}, Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}), so doubling $\tau$ -- this project's
own coarse robustness check -- flips it.

\paragraph{Where it is not: two distortion parameters per component.}
Placing all six declared distortion directions side by side -- two per component -- gives a
single six-column object. For $S_B$ it is \textbf{inseparable} at $\kappa=628.9$, rank $4$ of
$6$ (Figure~\ref{fig:spectrum}): not an unresolved estimator (worst singular-value variation
across the plateau, $1.023$ against an admissible $2$); not a threshold artifact (inseparable
across $\tau$ from $0.005$ to $1.0$, with no tolerance at which the object is both separable
and affordable); and not structural ($d=10\ge6$). Both near-null directions are
cross-mechanism, naming progression together with observation, with transmission carrying
under $5\%$ of the energy in either (Figure~\ref{fig:confound}, Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}):
a drifting removal hazard is nearly indistinguishable from a constant hazard change combined
with a drifting reporting rate. The confound needs the progression component to carry two
distortion parameters at once, exactly why it cannot arise under our one-parameter-per-component
scope, and exactly why that restriction -- not a bound on $\kappa$ -- is what the assumption
has to be. In the six-column case the spectrum matches the gapless, no-clean-break picture the
sloppy-models literature describes \citep{Gutenkunst_2007} (spread $2.80$ decades, gap
prominence $1.40$, $\tau\sigma_1$ falling inside the spectrum); in the three-column case it does
not. The reassurance a three-column threshold offers is a fact about having three columns, not
about the simulator. ($K$ denotes the simulator's three components throughout; the number of
distortion columns is a separate quantity, three or six depending on how many parameters each
component carries, and is written out in words rather than symbolically to avoid the two being
conflated.)

\section{Where exact conditional attribution becomes unaffordable, and why}
\label{sec:negative-result}

Composing Dufour's maximized Monte Carlo with rejection-sampling calibration
(Section~\ref{sec:background}) turns the diagnostic's identifiable case into a candidate for
exact conditional inference on which component gets attributed: comfortably affordable at a
known parameter, but unable to terminate once the nuisance parameters are only known within a
realistic neighborhood. The boundary of that failure is predicted, before it is measured, from
the ratio of nuisance-induced shift to observation noise, and the prediction holds -- and the
neighborhood at which it takes over is not an assumption we chose: it is narrower than the
uncertainty a maximum-likelihood fit to the simulator's own data reports, on every one of the
five nuisance coordinates. The composition controls the selective type-I error at the nominal
level conservatively rather than exactly, since it inherits both the supremum-over-nuisance-space
and the discrete-Monte-Carlo sources of conservativeness, without ever characterizing the
selection event analytically; its only theorem is a short exchangeability argument requiring the
selection rule to be a fixed, deterministic function of the data, holding only if every
admissible nuisance value keeps the observed selection event reachable at all. Every tool
composed here is established exact-inference machinery (Section~\ref{sec:background}); we
measure the composition against our own simulator.

\paragraph{Affordable at a known parameter, unbounded over a data-implied nuisance set.}
At the true parameter the composition is comfortably affordable: the worst selection cell
holds $23\%$ of null draws, and one test costs $4.2\times10^5$ to $4.3\times10^7$ simulator
draws against a pre-registered gate of $10^8$ -- a clear pass. The question is whether that
affordability survives once the nuisance parameters (transmission and recovery rates, reporting
fraction, initial infections, observation noise scale) are only known to the precision a real
analyst's own data would give them, rather than to an arbitrary assumed tolerance. We answer
this directly rather than assume a box: fitting the simulator's own model class to one realized
dataset by maximum likelihood and building a Bonferroni-corrected $95\%$ confidence box from
the resulting Fisher information gives relative half-widths from $2.3\%$, the
tightest-identified coordinate, to $16.6\%$, the loosest -- every one wider than the $\pm0.5\%$
box already known to break the composition (below). Re-measuring the gate inside this
data-implied box, the acceptance probability collapses to $10^{-5}$ at the primary case and to
exactly zero at the other three declared family-and-variant combinations, and the gate
\textbf{fails at every corner under all four}: the cheapest declared corner alone needs
$9.9\times10^9$ draws, essentially two orders of magnitude over budget even before the
confidence interval is taken into account. The collapse below was measured under a round,
assumed $\pm5\%$ box; here it survives, by a wide margin, under the box the data themselves
imply.

\paragraph{The mechanism, and the shape of the boundary.}
The selection rule's fixed-function requirement means it cannot track $\theta$; the nuisance
parameters shift the normalized summary vector by an amount growing with how far $\theta$ is
allowed to range, while a single draw's noise magnitude stays fixed. Once that shift exceeds
the noise, a $\theta$-free rule selects one component deterministically and the cell the data
actually selected becomes unreachable. A sweep over ten relative half-widths from $0.1\%$ to
$5\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:nontermination}) locates the shape of this collapse under the earlier,
round-number box: it is gradual rather than abrupt (about one decade of acceptance probability
lost per $0.7\%$ of relative nuisance error, log-linear at $R^2=0.94$), and the pre-registered
cost gate passes at every declared corner only inside a $\pm0.5\%$ box on all five nuisance
coordinates simultaneously -- an order of magnitude tighter than the data-implied box above
actually supports. The ratio of nuisance shift to observation noise is a one-line check a
reader with a different simulator can run before attempting this composition, and it is the
most transportable part of this result.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}[topsep=1pt,itemsep=0pt,parsep=0pt,leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{The scope assumption is not a formality.} Every positive result here holds under
the assumption, stated in Section~\ref{sec:method}, that each component carries at most one
distortion parameter -- a real restriction the results depend on, not a conservative formality
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}):
the moment one component carries two, the same summary set that separated all three under
every one-parameter assignment we tried becomes inseparable on a real confound. We have not
tested compound misspecification within a single mechanism -- two distortions of the
\emph{same} component rather than of two different ones.
\item \textbf{The diagnostic's own status.} The rank-and-condition-number screen
(Section~\ref{sec:method}) is prior art; the one seam it leaves unoccupied -- carrying an
estimated simulation-noise level forward into the rank tolerance -- is left open rather than
filled, since we derive our tolerance from a compute budget instead. The tolerance is fixed
before any singular value in this paper existed, so nothing here depends on filling that gap;
a reader adapting the diagnostic where the noise level binds needs to fill it themselves.
\item \textbf{A local diagnostic, at one parameter point.} $J$ is a linearization at $\eta=0$
and at the single parameter point $\theta_0$; separability of the $10\%$ perturbation our scale
represents is not implied by local linear separability, and we have not measured the diagnostic
at any other $\theta$. We report this as a stated limitation rather than as a tested one.
\item \textbf{One simulator, one scope of family sets, two hand-placed annotations.} Every
result here is measured on a single simulator under two distortion family sets chosen by us --
one deliberately adversarial; the eight assignments (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) are every
combination those sets permit, not a sample of families in general, and transport to other
simulator classes is untested. Two figures also carry hand-placed annotations outside the
automated check -- the rank on Figure~\ref{fig:spectrum}, the separability labels on
Figure~\ref{fig:assignments} -- both verified restatements, not independent claims.
\item \textbf{No learned component.} $S_A$ and $S_B$ are domain-chosen, not learned; a neural
posterior estimator or a learned summary statistic would sit in exactly the place $s(y)$ sits
here, and the diagnostic applies unchanged to its Jacobian -- the screen asks whether a summary
map separates components, and does not care whether a person or a network chose the map.
Whether a learned map would separate these components better or worse than $S_A$/$S_B$ do is
untested.
\end{itemize}
